# Editing grammar

Use this reference to make decisions, not to reproduce a fixed template.

## Rhythm hierarchy

Think in four levels:

1. **Track:** the full energy arc.
2. **Phrase:** a musical idea, commonly several bars.
3. **Beat:** the main pulse.
4. **Transient:** a short percussive or impact event.

Tie section changes to phrases, main cuts to selected beats, and very short bursts to transients. A transient is an opportunity, not an obligation.

## Cadence shapes

- **Confidence:** longer readable shots followed by one decisive change.
- **Acceleration:** progressively shorter cut lengths approaching a peak.
- **Pulse:** alternate one-beat and two-beat holds.
- **Burst and release:** a short rapid run followed by a long clean shot.
- **Counter-rhythm:** place a few cuts between expected beats, then resolve on a strong accent.
- **Silence reveal:** remove or thin the music before the title card.

Choose one or two dominant cadence shapes. Do not use every pattern in one short piece.

## Shot relationships

Strong adjacent cuts share or deliberately oppose a feature:

- movement direction;
- subject scale;
- composition geometry;
- brightness or dominant color;
- camera movement;
- semantic action;
- visual density.

Plan the relationship before generating the clips when possible.

## Layout choices

Multi-panel layouts are optional.

- **Two panels:** comparison or call-and-response.
- **Three panels:** escalating variation or central emphasis.
- **Four panels:** simultaneous breadth; requires clean crops and distinct sources.
- **Bands:** useful for horizontal motion and texture, weak for faces unless carefully framed.
- **Columns:** useful for vertical subjects, weak for wide environmental action.

If panel content changes on a beat, update the source media. A location swap alone is not a content cut.

## Transitions

Use hard cuts by default. Use a transition only when its geometry, motion, or light is already present in both shots. Keep it shorter than the thought it connects.

Avoid:

- generic crossfades;
- glow or lens-flare overlays without a source;
- arbitrary masks and borders;
- faux film burns;
- blur that exists before movement begins or after movement ends.

## Motion blur

For transformed layers, derive blur from frame-to-frame displacement and shutter exposure. For generated video, preserve native motion blur unless retiming creates stutter. Optical-flow interpolation can help moderate retimes but must be checked for warping.

Never substitute a static Gaussian blur animation for motion blur.
